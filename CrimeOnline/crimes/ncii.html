Overview

“The internet doesn’t forget. A private moment shared in trust becomes a permanent, uncontrollable public record. The image is gone — but it never really leaves.”

Cyber Civil Rights Initiative

Non-Consensual Intimate Image Sharing (NCII) — commonly referred to as ‘revenge porn’, though that term is widely rejected as inaccurate and victim-blaming — describes the distribution of sexually explicit or intimate images and videos of a person without their consent. It is one of the most rapidly expanding categories of internet crime, enabled by the same digital infrastructure that connects the modern world.

The term ‘revenge porn’ is misleading on two counts: ‘revenge’ implies the victim provoked the act, and ‘porn’ implies the content is consensual adult material. Neither is true. The preferred terminology reflects what this crime actually is: a violation of privacy, consent, and bodily autonomy, perpetrated through technology.

This report examines NCII in full depth: how it is defined, how it is committed, how it is experienced by victims, how different countries have legislated against it, and how the emergence of AI-generated deepfake imagery has fundamentally changed the threat landscape.

Section 2

Defining NCII: What It Is,
and What It Is Not

NCII encompasses any act of sharing, publishing, threatening to share, or possessing with intent to distribute intimate or sexually explicit images or videos of another person without their informed and ongoing consent.

→
Crucially: consent to create an image does not equal consent to distribute it. A photograph taken privately within a consensual relationship remains private. Its later distribution by either party without renewed consent constitutes NCII — regardless of who took the photograph.
01 — Most Common

Intimate Partner Distribution

The historically most prevalent form: a current or former romantic partner distributes images originally shared in the context of trust and intimacy. Often used as a tool of coercive control — the threat of distribution wielded even before any image is actually shared. Breakups are a primary trigger, but so are financial disputes, jealousy, and power dynamics.

High Prevalence
02 — Growing Threat

Hacking & Non-Consensual Recording

Images obtained entirely without the victim’s knowledge: through phishing and iCloud/Google Photos hacking, installation of hidden cameras, or recording during assaults. The 2014 ‘Celebgate’ hack — 100+ celebrities targeted via iCloud phishing — brought this variant to global attention.

Technical Intrusion
03 — Financially Motivated

Sextortion

An offender possesses (or claims to possess) intimate material and uses it to extort money, further images, or sexual compliance. Organised criminal networks specifically target teenagers and young adults across international borders. The FBI reports it has become one of the fastest-growing online crimes targeting minors.

Criminal Enterprise
04 — Fastest Growing

AI-Generated Deepfake NCII

Synthetic but realistic intimate imagery created using a real person’s face or likeness — without any genuine intimate material ever existing. Requires only social media photographs and freely available software. Any person with a public digital footprint is now a potential target. Examined fully in Section 5.

Emerging Threat
Section 3

Scale and Prevalence

Reliable data on NCII is difficult to obtain due to significant underreporting — many victims choose not to report out of shame, fear of further exposure, or lack of confidence that authorities will take meaningful action. Nevertheless, the research that exists is striking.

Between 4% and 10% of internet-using adults across Australia, Canada, and the EU report having been victims of NCII. Rates are substantially higher among women aged 18–34 and LGBTQ+ individuals, who face the additional threat of ‘outing’ being weaponised alongside image distribution.

Young people are especially vulnerable. The Thorn organisation found that 1 in 5 young adults aged 18–24 in the US had had intimate images shared without consent. For those under 18, such material simultaneously constitutes NCII and child sexual abuse material (CSAM).

!
UK Data: The Revenge Porn Helpline handled over 18,000 cases in 2022 — a fivefold increase from its first year of operation in 2015. This growth reflects both rising incidence and rising awareness.

Section 4

How NCII Is Perpetrated

Understanding how perpetrators acquire and distribute intimate material is essential to understanding prevention and enforcement.

1

Consensual Creation Within Relationships

Most NCII material was originally created consensually — shared between partners in a relationship of trust. The act becomes criminal only when one party later distributes it without renewed consent.

2

Hacking and Credential Theft

Personal devices, cloud accounts (iCloud, Google Photos, Dropbox), and email are targeted via phishing, spyware, and social engineering. Entire collections can be exfiltrated in minutes once access is obtained.

3

Hidden Recording

Concealed cameras planted in private spaces — changing rooms, bathrooms, bedrooms. South Korea’s ‘molka’ epidemic recorded 5,500+ police cases in a single year, with researchers estimating the true figure far higher.

4

AI Synthetic Generation

Facial photographs from social media are fed into generative AI tools to create fabricated intimate content. No genuine material required. Covered fully in Section 5.

5

Distribution Channels

Material is sent directly to victims’ employers, families, or communities; posted publicly on dedicated NCII sites or image boards; spread via Telegram channels; or sold on dark web marketplaces.

Section 5 — Feature Deep-Dive

The AI Deepfake Crisis

Deepfakes are synthetic media — images, videos, or audio — where a person’s likeness is digitally placed onto content they never created or participated in. The term combines ‘deep learning’ (the AI technology) and ‘fake’. While the technology has legitimate creative applications, it has been weaponised as a tool of sexual harassment at a genuinely alarming scale.

The creation of deepfake intimate imagery requires no technical expertise with modern consumer tools. Free applications can generate convincing synthetic explicit images using only a handful of source photographs — the kind available on any public social media profile — in under five minutes.

Deepfake NCII differs from conventional NCII in one especially troubling way: there is no original intimate material to take down. The image never existed — it was created. This makes both detection and legal redress dramatically more complicated.

!
2023–2024: Multiple documented cases emerged of AI-generated intimate images targeting teenage girls at schools across the United States and Europe. Generated using school social media photographs. These cases triggered emergency legislative responses in several US states.

Detection is technically complex. AI-detection tools identify inconsistencies in lighting, skin texture, and pixel patterns — but their accuracy erodes as generative AI improves. Detection technology is widely considered to lag behind generation technology by a significant margin.

Section 6

Impact on Victims

The psychological impact of NCII is profound, well-documented, and often severely underestimated by those who have not experienced it.

🧠

Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

Victims frequently describe intrusive thoughts, hypervigilance, emotional numbing, and dissociation — all consistent with PTSD. The loss of control over one’s own body and image is experienced as a distinct, acute trauma. Unlike a physical assault, NCII does not end.

💔

Depression and Severe Anxiety

High rates of clinical depression, generalised anxiety disorder, and panic attacks are documented among NCII victims — often persisting for years. The unpredictability of when and where the material might resurface means recovery is constantly interrupted.

⚠️

Suicidal Ideation and Self-Harm

Multiple independent studies find that a significant proportion of NCII victims experience suicidal thoughts. A number of documented cases have ended in suicide, particularly among younger victims. This is not an edge case — it is a recognised and recurring outcome.

🏢

Reputational and Professional Harm

Victims report job losses following employer discovery of material, expulsion from educational institutions, and ostracism from communities. Women face significantly harsher social judgement than male victims, with blame frequently directed at them for having created the images.

💰

Economic Consequences

Beyond lost income, victims face financial costs from legal action, content removal services, specialist technical assistance, and psychological care — costs that many cannot absorb, particularly without adequate civil legal remedies.

♾️

The Permanent Nature of Digital Distribution

Unlike a physical violation, NCII can surface and resurface indefinitely — discovered by new people, on new platforms, years after the original distribution. Victims describe living with perpetual, unresolvable vulnerability. There is rarely a clean ending.

Section 7

Legal Frameworks Around the World

The criminalisation of NCII has accelerated significantly since 2015, driven by sustained advocacy from victim support organisations and academics. The legal landscape remains highly uneven globally.

🇬🇧
United Kingdom

Online Safety Act 2023

Sharing intimate images without consent is now a specific criminal offence in England and Wales, carrying a potential custodial sentence. Deepfake intimate image creation — not just distribution — is also criminalised, one of the first laws to do so. Earlier groundwork was laid by the Voyeurism (Offences) Act 2019.

Comprehensive Coverage
🇺🇸
United States

State-by-State Framework

The US has no federal NCII criminal statute. The SHIELD Act has stalled in Congress multiple times due to First Amendment concerns. As of 2024, 48 states have enacted their own NCII laws — varying substantially in scope, definitions, and penalties. Cross-jurisdictional cases face significant enforcement gaps.

Partial — No Federal Law
🇪🇺
European Union

Digital Services Act 2024

The DSA, in full force from 2024, imposes substantial obligations on large online platforms to proactively detect and remove illegal content — including NCII. Member states are also implementing the EU Victims’ Rights Directive. National criminal laws still vary widely across member states.

Platform Obligations — Patchy Criminal Law
🇦🇺
Australia

Online Safety Act 2021

Australia’s framework is among the most comprehensive globally. The eSafety Commissioner — an independent government authority — has powers to compel platforms to remove NCII content within 24 hours of a valid complaint. Criminal penalties are established at state and territory level.

One of the World’s Strongest Frameworks

Why Prosecutions Remain Difficult

Even in jurisdictions with strong laws, securing convictions is hard. Perpetrators use pseudonyms and anonymising technology; cases cross multiple jurisdictions with unclear authority; digital evidence is volatile and quickly deleted; specialist cybercrime police units are under-resourced; and many victims withdraw due to psychological toll or fear of further exposure during trial.

Section 8

Platform Responses

Technology platforms have progressively strengthened NCII policies under regulatory pressure — but the gap between policy and enforcement remains substantial.

Hash-Matching TechnologyTechnical solution

A ‘hash’ is a unique digital fingerprint generated from an image file. If a hash is generated from an original NCII image, any subsequent upload of that identical image can be automatically detected and blocked before it reaches anyone. StopNCII.org allows victims to generate hashes without uploading the images themselves. Used by tens of thousands of victims globally as of 2024.

Major Platform PoliciesPolicy enforcement

Meta, Google, Microsoft, Snapchat, and others now explicitly prohibit NCII in community standards and have implemented victim reporting mechanisms. However, enforcement is largely reactive — relying on victims to first discover the material and then report it, often at significant personal cost.

The Telegram ProblemPersistent gap

Telegram applies minimal content moderation and permits large public channels with no oversight. Research by the Internet Watch Foundation and Stanford Internet Observatory has documented channels with tens of thousands of subscribers openly hosting non-consensual content. The 2024 arrest of Telegram founder Pavel Durov in France on charges related to platform facilitation of criminal activity represents a significant escalation of regulatory pressure.

Section 9

Prevention and Support

Primary responsibility for NCII rests entirely with perpetrators. The following practices can reduce individual vulnerability and help victims access appropriate support.

For Personal Protection

✓
Use strong, unique passwords for all accounts containing personal data. Enable multi-factor authentication wherever available — especially on cloud photo services.
✓
Review smartphone backup settings periodically. Many devices automatically sync all photos to cloud services — check that this is intentional and the account is properly secured.
✓
Exercise caution sharing intimate images even with trusted partners. Once shared, an image cannot be unshared. Open conversation about expectations — including what happens if a relationship ends — is a protective factor.
✓
Recognise sextortion tactics: unsolicited contact from strangers online that rapidly escalates to intimacy is a documented pattern used by criminal networks. Claims of possessing webcam footage are frequently false but are standard sextortion leverage.
✓
Be aware that AI deepfakes require only facial photographs — restricting who can see your photos on social media reduces (though does not eliminate) exposure to synthetic NCII.
