Prevention &
What's Being Done

Across six categories of internet crime, governments, platforms, law enforcement agencies, and individuals are responding — with uneven effectiveness, insufficient resources, and hard structural limits. This page synthesises what is working, what is not, and what you can do.

Overview

"The internet was built for openness. Retrofitting it for safety is one of the defining governance challenges of the 21st century — and we are still losing."

Marietje Schaake, International Policy Director, Stanford Cyber Policy Center, 2023

Across all six categories examined in this project, a consistent pattern emerges: the harms produced by internet crime are real, documented, and severe; the legal and technical responses are real but insufficient; and the structural conditions that enable these crimes — platform architectures optimised for engagement over safety, jurisdictional fragmentation, inadequate enforcement resources, and the pace of technological change — remain largely intact.

This does not mean nothing is working. Significant progress has been made. The EU's Digital Services Act represents the most ambitious attempt yet to hold platforms legally accountable for the harms their systems produce. Hash-matching technology has meaningfully reduced NCII distribution on participating platforms. Major ransomware operations have been disrupted by coordinated international law enforcement. Hundreds of cybercriminals are prosecuted every year.

But progress is incremental against an adaptive adversary, and in most categories the trajectory of harm continues upward. The $12.5 billion in US cybercrime losses in 2023 was a record high. NCII volumes, including AI-generated deepfakes, continue to grow. The online drug trade has fragmented into a form more resilient to enforcement than Silk Road ever was. Hate speech radicalisation pipelines operate today with greater reach than at any previous point.

Understanding the shape of the response — what is working, what is not, and where the hardest limits lie — is as important as understanding the crimes themselves.

Section 4

Law Enforcement:
What Works and What Doesn't

🌐

Europol's EC3 — European Cybercrime Centre

Europol's dedicated cybercrime unit coordinates investigations across EU member states, provides technical support and cyber intelligence, and runs joint operations with non-EU partners including the FBI. Responsible for significant takedowns including AlphaBay, Hansa, Hydra, and LockBit. The scale of resources, however, remains vastly smaller than the scale of the problem.

🔍

FBI Cyber Division and IC3

The FBI's dedicated cyber division runs major investigations including Operation Bayonet, the Colonial Pipeline ransomware response, and major BEC prosecutions. IC3 (Internet Crime Complaint Center) aggregates victim reports into actionable intelligence. The FBI has also pioneered the use of blockchain analytics as a primary investigative tool — recovering hundreds of millions in cryptocurrency seized from criminal wallets.

🤝

International Cooperation — The Budapest Convention

The Budapest Convention on Cybercrime (2001) is the primary international legal framework enabling cross-border cybercrime cooperation — mutual legal assistance treaties (MLATs), joint investigations, and evidence sharing. Signed by 68 countries, it provides the legal basis for most successful international prosecutions. However, major cybercrime-producing nations including Russia and China are not signatories, creating significant enforcement gaps for the most damaging state-adjacent criminal operations.

⚖️

The Persistent Limits

Despite genuine operational successes, three structural limits consistently constrain law enforcement effectiveness: attribution (identifying perpetrators through layers of anonymity technology remains technically and legally complex); jurisdiction (criminals deliberately operate from non-cooperative or permissive states); and resources (most national police forces lack the specialist technical expertise to investigate sophisticated cybercrime at scale). These limits are not administrative failures — they are structural features of how the internet was built.

Section 5

Your Personal Protection Guide:
By Topic

The most effective individual defence against internet crime is informed behaviour. The key actions per topic are summarised here — full detail is available on each topic page.

01

NCII & Deepfakes

Full report →
Use strong, unique passwords and MFA on all cloud photo storage — iCloud, Google Photos, Dropbox.
Review social media privacy settings — restrict who can see tagged photos and location data.
If targeted: preserve evidence immediately. Report to StopNCII.org, your national helpline, and police. Do not delete content before documenting it.
Awareness: AI deepfakes require only your face photos. Restricting public photo access reduces (but cannot eliminate) exposure to synthetic NCII.
02

Online Drug Trade

Full report →
Counterfeit pill awareness: pills purchased online — including through apparent acquaintances — may contain fentanyl regardless of appearance. One pill can be lethal.
Fentanyl test strips and naloxone are harm reduction tools available in most countries. If you or someone you know uses drugs, access to naloxone saves lives.
Sextortion in drug contexts: blackmail is used alongside drug supply to extort continued payment. Report to police regardless of the illegality of the original purchase.
03

Piracy & Malware

Full report →
Never install cracked or pirated software. 58% of pirated software packages contain malware — ransomware, credential stealers, and remote access trojans are the most common payloads.
Keep operating systems and applications updated. The majority of malware — including that distributed via piracy — exploits known, patched vulnerabilities in unupdated software.
Use reputable antivirus software and enable real-time scanning. For work devices, install software only from approved, verified sources.
04

Hate Speech & Radicalisation

Full report →
Algorithmic awareness: understand that recommendation systems are optimised for engagement, not accuracy or safety. Deliberately seek out diverse sources rather than following algorithms.
Report hate speech on platforms — consistent reporting builds enforcement records and can trigger account actions. Document before reporting in case content is deleted.
If you're concerned about someone's radicalisation: contact Prevent (UK), Life After Hate (US), or equivalent national counter-extremism services for guidance before intervening directly.
05

Cyberstalking & Doxxing

Full report →
Submit opt-out requests to major data brokers (Spokeo, Whitepages, BeenVerified). Use services like DeleteMe to automate this across dozens of sites.
Audit all shared accounts and smart home devices after any relationship change. Revoke access immediately — stalkerware may still be active on shared devices.
If targeted: document everything with timestamps. Contact the National Stalking Helpline (UK), CCRI (US), or equivalent national service. Do not confront the perpetrator directly.
06

Financial Cybercrime

Full report →
Enable multi-factor authentication on every financial account and email address. This single step prevents the majority of account takeover attacks.
Verify before acting on any unexpected request for money or credentials — through a separate, trusted channel. No legitimate bank or company will pressure you to act immediately.
Online investment opportunities offering unusually high returns, guaranteed profits, or involving cryptocurrency — especially those from recent contacts — are almost certainly fraud.
Report financial cybercrime immediately: FBI IC3 (ic3.gov) in the US; Action Fraud (actionfraud.police.uk) in the UK. Speed of reporting is critical for financial recovery.
Section 6

Why This Is Hard:
Six Structural Challenges

Understanding why internet crime is so difficult to combat requires looking beyond individual failures of law, platform, or enforcement — and examining the structural features of the internet itself that create this environment.

01

Jurisdictional Fragmentation

The internet is global; law is national. A criminal operating from a non-cooperative state, hosting content on servers in a third country, and targeting victims in a fourth faces limited legal exposure regardless of the severity of the harm caused. Closing this gap requires treaties, political will, and enforcement resources that are all in short supply.

02

Pace of Technological Change

Law, regulation, and enforcement practice move slowly. Technology moves fast. AI-generated NCII and voice-cloning fraud barely existed as legal categories two years ago. By the time legislation catches up with a new technology, the technology has typically advanced further. Regulatory lag is not a failure of governance — it is a structural feature of how democratic lawmaking works.

03

Encryption and Anonymity

The same technologies that protect journalists, dissidents, and ordinary people from surveillance — end-to-end encryption, VPNs, Tor, privacy coins — are the primary tools of cybercriminals. There is no technical solution that protects one without protecting the other. The encryption debate is not a question with a right answer; it is a genuine value conflict between privacy and safety.

04

Platform Incentive Misalignment

Platforms are primarily commercial entities optimised for engagement and growth. Content moderation is expensive, politically controversial, and reduces the engagement metrics that drive revenue. Platforms invest in safety when regulators require it, when reputational costs make inaction too expensive, or when it aligns with commercial interests. Left to their own devices, most platforms have historically underinvested in safety relative to the scale of harm produced.

05

Underreporting and Evidence Loss

The majority of internet crime goes unreported — through shame, hopelessness about recovery, or lack of awareness of reporting channels. Without victim reports, law enforcement cannot build the intelligence picture needed to identify and prosecute perpetrators. And digital evidence is highly volatile: platforms delete content, accounts are closed, and cached material disappears, often before victims know they need to preserve it.

06

Adversarial Adaptation

Criminal operations adapt. Every successful takedown generates knowledge about enforcement methods. Every new platform policy drives criminals to less-regulated alternatives. Every new authentication method prompts new bypass techniques. This is not a problem that can be solved — it is an adversarial dynamic that must be managed. The goal is not eradication but sufficient friction to reduce harm, deter casual participation, and protect the most vulnerable.

Conclusion

Awareness Is the
First Line of Defence

The crimes covered in this project are not abstract risks. They affect ordinary people — students, parents, professionals, activists, journalists — every day. Understanding how they work is the necessary first step to protecting yourself, recognising them in others, and contributing to a more informed public conversation about how the internet should be governed.
